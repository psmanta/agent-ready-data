
**An eighth tool — needed earlier than planned.** The Forward-Looking Note above named Cochran's Q as something H7's breadth ladder would eventually need (more than two paired conditions). It turned out H2's magnitude ladder (5%, 15%, 40%, 100% — also four paired conditions per field, same population) needed it first.

| Question | Naive first choice | Why it's more subtle than it looks | What we use instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does drift rate differ anywhere across a >2-level paired ladder (e.g. magnitude 5/15/40/100%) for the same field? | Running all pairwise McNemar's directly (6 tests for k=4) | Multiple comparisons inflate the family-wise error rate; unlike H1's unordered field set, the ladder IS ordered, so an omnibus gate followed by adjacent-step tests is both more rigorous and more interpretable | **Cochran's Q** (chi-square approximation) as an omnibus gate — proceed to adjacent-step McNemar's ONLY if significant, localizing where in the ladder the change occurs. If not significant, the null finding is reported directly, same discipline as H8b's conditional generation rule. |

**A note on exact vs. approximate here specifically, since it looks inconsistent with the rest of this table at first glance:** every other "prefer exact" choice in this document corrected for a SMALL number of runs or observations (5 baseline runs, 5-vs-10 Jaccard scores). Cochran's Q's asymptotic validity depends on the number of *subjects* (~1,000 customers), which is large — the chi-square approximation is well-behaved at that scale. No readily-available exact permutation version exists without adding a new dependency (statsmodels) for a correction our actual sample size doesn't need.

**Verification method, worth noting since it differs from every other primitive's approach:** rather than checking against a recalled reference number, Cochran's Q was verified against a proven mathematical identity — when k=2, it must algebraically reduce exactly to McNemar's uncorrected chi-square statistic, (b-c)²/(b+c). Confirmed by hand derivation and numerically (both sides agreed to 9 decimal places on synthetic data). Additionally verified against a "cliff-then-plateau" scenario (sharp jump at one step, flat elsewhere): the omnibus test fired correctly (p≈10⁻⁶³) and the adjacent-step follow-ups correctly localized the effect to the single true step, finding nothing at the two flat steps (p=0.80, p=0.21) — confirming the gate doesn't mask or dilute a genuine 1a-style cliff finding.

**A note on tie-handling in the descriptive trend correlation, verified with a worked example rather than assumed correct.** Every prior test of `descriptive_trend_correlation()` used tie-free data. A hand-worked example with genuine ties (Study Hours vs. Exam Scores, n=5, two tied pairs) surfaced a real, non-obvious distinction worth documenting: the classic textbook shortcut formula for Spearman's rho, $1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}$, is only an *approximation* when ties are present. It gives 0.925 on the worked example. The mathematically correct method — Pearson correlation computed directly on the tie-averaged ranks — gives 0.9211. `scipy.stats.spearmanr` (what our wrapper calls) uses the correct method, confirmed by computing all three ways independently and checking they agree exactly (0.9210526315789475 for both the hand-derived Pearson-on-ranks calculation and scipy's output). This matters for H2/H7's real usage, not just the abstract case — a magnitude or breadth ladder's drift rates could plausibly tie exactly (e.g. two levels plateauing at the same rate), and the shortcut formula would silently misstate the curve's shape in that case.
